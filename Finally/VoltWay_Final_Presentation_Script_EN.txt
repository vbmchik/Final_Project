2. TREND AND VEHICLE TYPES | 0:55–2:10 | 75 seconds

[Show Power BI page 1: EV Curve & Market Leaders. Start with all filters cleared.]

First, separate new registrations from the running total. The total can rise even when fewer vehicles are added each quarter.

In our sample, fully electric registrations fell from about 22,000 in 2025 Q3 to about 10,800 in 2026 Q3, a fall of about 51 percent. The dashboard line shows their share: 25.1 percent in 2024 Q1 and 13.3 percent in 2026 Q3.

Now look at the vehicle-type chart. In 2026 Q3, ordinary hybrids account for 35.7 percent and plug-in hybrids for 26.5 percent. Together, that is 62.2 percent.

We must keep these groups separate when considering charging. Ordinary hybrids do not plug in. Plug-in hybrid sales alone do not establish demand for our DC sites.

The trend uses full quarters through 2026 Q3. Our registry sample covers vehicles made in 2024–2026, rather than the full national fleet.

Drivers may charge away from their home district, so traffic can help us choose corridors to study.

We checked a CBS table covering 35 selected road sections. One section of Road 431 has about 164,000 vehicle passes per day in 2024.

These counts cover all vehicle types. The same car may pass several sections. The figures help us identify roads to investigate, but do not predict charging customers at an individual site.

6. WHAT WE MUST CHECK AT EACH SITE | 5:15–5:50 | 35 seconds

Before choosing an address, we need to know how many electric cars pass or stop there, how long drivers stay, and whether they can charge at home or work.

We also need actual sessions, energy sold, busy hours and queues at nearby chargers. Price, reliability and access may affect demand.

Finally, we must check parking and the electricity connection. These checks turn a district shortlist into evidence about a specific site.

7. MONEY: ONE SITE AND THE 50-SITE SCENARIO | 5:50–7:50 | 120 seconds

[Use the money slide, then the scenario chart on Power BI page 2.]

Our example site has four DC chargers and costs 680,000 shekels to start. Low, Base and High assume 12, 24 and 40 daily sessions for the whole site.

At full demand, in a 30-day month, operating cash is about minus 800 shekels in Low, plus 15,000 in Base and plus 36,000 in High.

About 13 sessions a day cover operating costs. About 22 meet a five-year simple payback target at full demand. This second calculation excludes the slow start.

The monthly model includes a slow start. Base pays back in month 51 and High in month 23. Low does not pay back within ten years.

The dashboard also scales the model to 50 sites. Initial investment is 34 million shekels. After five years, cumulative cash after that investment is about minus 38.2 million in Low, plus 7.6 million in Base and plus 68.7 million in High.

This scales the same site assumptions across all 50 sites. It does not model a separate demand level or a separate opening date for each site.

That wide range supports testing demand with a pilot. Costs use a 2020 benchmark, so we need current quotes. The model also excludes tax and financing.

8. HOW WE DID THE WORK | 7:50–8:20 | 30 seconds

We used Python to clean the data and SQL to compare quarters, brands, owners and districts.

A statistical test showed that the electric share differs across owner groups. It does not explain their charging habits.

Excel connects demand assumptions to cash flow and payback. Power BI presents the main findings.

Together, these tools help us move from raw records to a decision.

9. HOW WE USED AI, AND WHAT COMES NEXT | 8:20–9:20 | 60 seconds

We used AI to help build a first template for the financial model. We tried the template, checked its results and added what our project needed.

The useful result is a model we can update when we have better information. We can change demand, costs and other inputs, then compare the cases again.

With more data, AI could also help us build a model of electric vehicle traffic and possible charging demand. For that, we would need local traffic, the electric vehicle share, stop patterns and real charging use.

We would check its predictions against observed data. Then it could help us identify areas where chargers may be needed. Today, this is a possible next step, not a result we have already proved.

Our cumulative registration count is increasing. New fully electric registrations in recent quarters are weaker. Our sample cannot measure the full national fleet, including exits.

Which brand should be our partner?
We have a shortlist. Changing quarterly leaders and missing commercial information make a final choice premature.

Why can the running total grow while the trend falls?
We still add vehicles, but we may add fewer than before.

Can AI already tell us where to build?
Not from these data alone. A useful prediction needs more local data and checks against real observations.

Why does payback take 51 months rather than the simple calculation on the slide?
The monthly model includes lower demand during start-up.

ADDITIONAL Q&A FOR THE CURRENT DASHBOARD

Does the plan cut peak load by 21–34%?
The calculation reduces EVs per fast charging point by about 34% in Tel Aviv and 21% in Central, holding EV counts fixed. We have not modeled peak queues.

Why 28 / 18 / 4?
It is the team's illustrative allocation. Tel Aviv and Central rank highest on the all-EV ratio. Jerusalem ranks second on the private-EV ratio. These facts support investigation, not an optimal allocation.

Does +7.6 million mean annual profit?
No. It is modeled cumulative cash for 50 identical sites after five years, including subtraction of the initial investment, before tax and financing.

Why can filters disagree with chart titles?
Several titles contain fixed numbers. Clear filters before quoting those figures and verify dynamic-title or interaction behavior in Power BI before presenting.
